## 1. Executive Summary
The global landscape of electric energy is characterized by a significant ongoing transition, yet remains heavily reliant on fossil fuels, which still account for approximately 80% of global energy consumption and 66% of electricity generation [S1]. Empirical research highlights that residential electricity consumption is influenced by dwelling attributes, heating/cooling equipment, and occupant profiles, with varying patterns observed even within urban areas [S1]. A notable challenge is consumer misperception of energy usage and a preference for curtailment over efficiency measures [S1].

Mechanisms driving the energy transition include policy interventions, technological advancements, and the increasing adoption of distributed generation (DG) [S1]. DG, particularly from renewable sources, offers benefits like reduced power losses and improved supply security [S1]. Despite these drivers, significant debates persist regarding the optimal balance between centralized and decentralized energy systems, public acceptance of energy infrastructure near residential areas, and the existence of an 'energy efficiency gap' [S1]. Furthermore, ensuring energy justice and equity remains a critical challenge, as energy poverty and disparities in energy access continue to affect vulnerable populations [S1].

## 2. Key Findings
1. Despite global efforts towards renewable energy, fossil fuels continue to dominate both overall energy consumption (approximately 80%) and electricity generation (66%) [S1].
2. Residential electricity consumption is influenced by factors such as dwelling characteristics, the presence and use of heating/cooling systems, and occupant demographics like income and number of residents [S1].
3. Consumers often exhibit misperceptions regarding energy usage, overestimating the consumption of low-energy appliances and underestimating high-energy ones, and tend to favor reducing usage (curtailment) over implementing energy efficiency strategies [S1].
4. Investments in clean energy are on the rise globally, yet the development of green initiatives is frequently hindered by a lack of access to private financing, with substantial financing deficits projected to meet energy efficiency goals by 2030 [S1].
5. The proportion of electricity in final energy consumption is increasing, projected to rise from 21% in 2024 to approximately 30% by 2030 to align with net-zero emission targets, necessitating significant growth in electricity generation capacity in countries like the US [S1].

## 3. Evidence Analysis
The empirical evidence surrounding electric energy underscores a persistent reliance on fossil fuels, despite the ongoing energy transition [S1]. Approximately 80% of global energy and 66% of electricity generation are still derived from these sources, posing challenges related to pollution, climate change, and energy security [S1]. Research into residential consumption patterns reveals a nuanced picture, where factors such as dwelling age, size, the presence of HVAC systems, and occupant income and household size all play a role in electricity usage [S1]. Notably, consumers often demonstrate a misunderstanding of energy consumption by individual appliances, and a behavioral tendency to reduce usage rather than adopt efficiency measures [S1].

The mechanisms driving the shift towards cleaner electric energy are multifaceted. Policy interventions, such as China's dual-pilot energy policies, have demonstrated efficacy in improving urban environmental productivity through industrial and technological adjustments [S1]. Technological advancements, including smart grid technologies and distributed generation (DG), are crucial. DG, especially when integrated with renewables, enhances grid stability by reducing transmission losses and improving supply security [S1]. Furthermore, the energy transition itself has been shown to positively impact corporate performance by easing financing constraints and encouraging R&D investments, particularly in firms with greater market flexibility [S1]. The electrification trend is accelerating, with electricity's share in final energy consumption expected to grow significantly by 2030, a trend supported by policy, financial incentives, and technological progress [S1].

However, the transition is not without its debates and challenges. A fundamental tension exists between centralized and decentralized energy models, with ongoing discussions about the most effective technological pathways for decarbonization [S1]. Public opinion presents another hurdle: while renewable energy technologies are generally viewed positively, there is often resistance to their proximity to residential areas, highlighting a conflict between policy objectives and local acceptance [S1]. The concept of an 'energy efficiency gap'—where profitable energy-saving investments are not made—remains a subject of debate, with explanations ranging from high discount rates to behavioral factors [S1]. Limitations in evidence-based policy-making are also apparent, with systematic reviews showing mixed results for interventions like rural electrification, making policy interpretation difficult [S1]. Crucially, grid readiness is emerging as a bottleneck, requiring substantial investment in modernization to accommodate the increasing integration of intermittent renewable sources [S1]. Finally, issues of energy justice and equity are paramount, as energy poverty and disparities in access, often exacerbated by gender and ethnicity, persist, particularly in developing regions [S1].

## 4. Research Limitations & Conflicting Evidence
While empirical evidence offers valuable insights, several limitations and areas of conflicting information exist. The pervasive reliance on fossil fuels [S1] highlights a significant gap between current practices and transition goals, suggesting that the pace of change may be slower than desired. Consumer misperceptions and behavioral tendencies represent a challenge for implementing energy efficiency policies effectively [S1]. The debates surrounding centralized versus decentralized energy systems [S1] and the 'energy efficiency gap' [S1] indicate a lack of universal consensus on optimal strategies. Furthermore, the 'credibility revolution' in economics, while aiming for causal identification, has shown limitations, with systematic reviews yielding divided evidence on the impacts of certain interventions, such as rural electrification [S1]. This ambiguity poses challenges for policymakers. Finally, the shift of bottlenecks from generation to grids, storage, and integration [S1], along with persistent issues of energy justice and equity [S1], underscore the complexity and multi-faceted nature of the energy transition, requiring nuanced and context-specific solutions.
